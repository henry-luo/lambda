The quiet harbour: a walk along the old pier

By R. Mariner

The harbour wakes slowly. Long before the first ferry sounds its horn, the gulls have already claimed the railings of the old pier, and the fishermen who still keep boats here are untying ropes that their grandparents tied in the same knots. The planks are grey with salt and sun, and every third one has been replaced at some point in the last century, so that walking the length of the pier is like reading the rings of a tree laid flat.

At the far end stands the lamp, no longer lit, its glass long since replaced by a sheet of painted tin. Children dare each other to touch it. Adults lean against its base and look back at the town, which from here arranges itself into the picture on the postcards: the church, the three tall warehouses, the row of painted houses that the council repaints every spring in colours chosen by a committee nobody can name.

What the postcards do not show is the sound. The pier hums. Water moves under it in a rhythm that has nothing to do with the tide tables pinned to the harbourmaster's door, and the hum rises through the soles of your shoes until you stop noticing it, which is roughly the moment you realise you have been standing still for ten minutes watching a crab decide whether to cross a shadow.

Walk back slowly. The bakery on the corner opens at seven, and the first loaves are the ones worth queueing for. The woman who runs it will ask whether you went to the end, and whatever you answer, she will tell you that the lamp was lit until 1962, and that her father was the last to light it, and this is true.
